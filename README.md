### About the Experiment 

Generation of High-Voltage Impulse Waves via Marx Circuit focuses on designing and demonstrating a multistage impulse generator capable of producing sudden, short-duration high-voltage surges from a relatively low-voltage direct current (DC) supply. In power systems, electrical equipment such as transformers, insulators, and transmission cables are frequently exposed to natural lightning strikes and sudden switching surges. Before deploying this equipment in the field, engineers use a Marx generator in the laboratory to simulate these extreme voltage spikes and test whether the insulation can withstand them without failing.
Core Concept
Generating extremely high voltages directly using a single transformer and rectifier is bulky, expensive, and requires massive insulation. The Marx circuit solves this problem through a simple switching concept: it charges several capacitors slowly in parallel at a low voltage, and then discharges them rapidly in series. When connected in series, the individual voltages of all the capacitors add up, creating a massive, brief voltage pulse at the output.
Working Principle
Parallel Charging Phase
A standard DC power supply feeds current through a network of charging resistors into multiple capacitor stages arranged in parallel. During this slow charging process, small air gaps (called sphere spark gaps) between the stages act as open switches, keeping the stages separated until every capacitor is fully charged to the same level.
Cascade Triggering Phase
Once the capacitors are fully charged, the first spark gap is intentionally triggered to break down and conduct electricity. When this first gap sparks over, the air ionizes and acts like a closed switch. This sudden connection creates an overvoltage across the next spark gap, causing it to spark over as well. Like a row of falling dominoes, all the remaining spark gaps break down one after another in a fraction of a microsecond.
Series Discharge and Wave Shaping
When all the spark gaps fire simultaneously, the capacitors are instantly linked together in a single series chain. Because the charging resistors offer high resistance to sudden surges, the stored electrical energy bypasses the resistors and rushes straight through the ionized spark gaps toward the output. Before reaching the equipment under test, the surge passes through wave-shaping resistors that smooth the pulse into a standard impulse wave—characterized by a steep, rapid rise to peak voltage followed by a gradual decay.
Key Project Applications
High-Voltage Insulation Testing: Verifying the dielectric strength of power transformers, circuit breakers, bushings, and suspension insulators against simulated lightning strikes.
Surge Arrestor Calibration: Testing protective devices such as lightning arrestors to ensure they safely divert surge currents to the ground.
Pulsed Power Research: Supplying high-energy pulses for plasma physics experiments, X-ray generation, and laser excitation.
